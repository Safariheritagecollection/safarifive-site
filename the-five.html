The five

The five experiences that make a safari.

The Safari Five does not replace the Big Five. It adds what the animal list leaves out: the experiences that turn a good safari into one you never forget.

1

A true bushcamp

The foundation that makes everything else possible.

A bushcamp is a small, unfenced camp set deep in wild country, inside a national park or wildlife concession. It is a handful of rooms (often just three to six) built of thatch, reed, canvas and local timber, usually open to the bush, run off-grid and built around guiding rather than facilities. The animals move through camp, not around it. You fall asleep to hippos in the river and wake to elephants outside. It is smaller and closer to the wild than a lodge, and more comfortable than a camping trip where you pitch your own tent.

2

A walking safari

In a vehicle, you see Africa. On foot, you feel it.

A walking safari is a guided walk through big-game country, led by a licensed guide, usually with an armed scout or ranger. Walks typically start at first light and last three or four hours, at an easy pace, in a small group in single file.

More on the walking safari →

3

A conservation story you can see

Stronger communities. More wildlife.

Every wild place you visit is protected by someone: scouts on patrol, communities who live beside the animals, and the tourism that helps pay for both. Seeing that story for yourself, on a village or school visit, a talk with the scouts or a look at a water project, is part of the classic safari.

More on the conservation story →

4

The Big Three carnivores

Lion, leopard and wild dog, in one place.

The Big Three are Africa's three great apex hunters: lion, leopard and African wild dog. Many parks have one or two of them. Few have all three in numbers that give you a real chance of seeing each in a single stay.

More on the Big Three →

5

A night drive

Africa after dark, with a sundowner first.

Every great night drive starts with its opening act: the sundowner. The vehicle stops somewhere with a view, drinks come out, and you watch the sun go down over the bush. Then the spotlight comes on and the drive carries on into the dark, into the hours when the bush changes shift. Many national parks do not allow night drives, so where they are allowed, they are a privilege.
